The one that decided things: the trend reports agree that depth and gradient are back, but the useful version of that finding is narrower than it looks. A gradient earns its place when it is chromatically specific — many precise stops, grain, defined behaviour in motion, impossible to reproduce by accident. A two-stop blue with a visible seam is the opposite of that, and LogoLounge's own warning is not to slap a gradient on something and call it strategy. So: flat, one brand blue, and let the page carry the atmosphere.

5 Four directions

All four drop “.com”, go flat, and are set in Space Grotesk — the family the site is already locked to — so the logo and the headline finally share DNA. Each shows the lockup, the 32px mark, and the 16px favicon.

No pictorial mark at all. This is where the field has landed — icons secondary, the wordmark central, personality carried by the letterforms themselves.

The one engineered detail: of the two adjacent es, the first takes the brand blue. Two identical letterforms sitting side by side, one picked out — that is, without labouring it, what fitment looks like: the pair that matches. It's the disciplined version of the “one disrupted letter” trend, and it costs nothing structurally.

The favicon is ee, not m. An m in a blue square belongs to a hundred companies; nobody else's tab has “ee” in it. That's a real 16px asset — high contrast, two counters, reads at a glance.

Against it: it gives up a symbol, and symbols are what get printed on a shop's window sticker in Senen. If physical presence matters in year one, 5b or 5c serve you better.

Keeps the M you already have equity in, but rebuilds it from the wordmark's own letterforms instead of a separate illustration — and cuts a keyway into it, so the M's centre seats into a slot. Two forms that only go together one way.

This is the current trend done with discipline: the expressive initial built from the same structural DNA as the rest of the type, so it can stand alone or slot back into the lockup without breaking the read. The present mark fails exactly that test — it looks like it wandered in from another brand.

The notch is a 32px-and-up detail. I tried it at 16 and it fails — at that size the bar eats the M's centre vertex and both counters, and the tile reads as an indistinct blue chip. Which is the same failure this document diagnoses as problem 01, so the honest answer is a tiered mark: notch above 32px, bare M at 16. That's the cited standard anyway — a favicon is an adaptation, not a shrunken lockup.

Against it: “letter in a rounded square” is the most crowded shape in software branding. The keyway is what saves it, and the keyway is subtle.

Two brackets close around the name. Registration marks — the notation an engineer uses to say this, exactly this, within tolerance.

The reason I like it: it isn't a logo, it's a grammar. The same brackets can close around a part name, a spec, a verified fit — as shown at the bottom of the dark panel. The brand becomes a gesture the product performs, not a badge stuck in the corner. And it's the same vocabulary as scan direction 4d, so motion and identity would agree.

At 16px it's four strokes — which is the fewest marks of any direction here, but also the thinnest. My first pass at 1.5px hairlines was visibly the faintest of the three favicons, so the tile above is a proper adaptation: strokes thickened to 2px and the pair widened to fill 12 of the 16px. Even so, this is the one I'd want to see on a real tab strip beside 5a's “ee” before committing — line weight at 16px is where bracket marks usually lose.

Against it: brackets are unowned by nature. It only becomes yours through relentless use, which takes discipline the company may not have bandwidth for in launch year. It also reads more “developer tool” than “trusted marketplace” — worth testing on shop owners before committing.

What I'd do, and what I'd need

5a, with 5c's brackets adopted as a UI device rather than part of the logo. You get a wordmark that shares DNA with every headline on the site, a favicon nobody else has, and a bracket grammar the product can perform — without asking a symbol to carry meaning it can't hold at 16px.

Whichever you take, “.com” comes out. That's the one non-negotiable. It dates the brand, it's redundant next to the URL bar, the deck and the footer both call the company “Mobeeli”, and the primary surface is becoming company.mobeeli.com — so the logo is asserting a domain that isn't even the main one.

To take any of this past mockup: the vector source of the current mark (I only have two flattened PNGs), and a note on where it appears physically today. If it's already on shop windows or printed material, that changes my recommendation toward 5d and I'd want to know before you decide.
